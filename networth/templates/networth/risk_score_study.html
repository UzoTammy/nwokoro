1. What the score means

The risk score is the fraction of the USD value of your assets that could be lost in a bad year, meaning a year bad enough to happen about once in 20 years. It runs from 0 (nothing at risk) to 1 (everything lost).

It reads as a plain sentence: “In a 1-in-20 bad year, about X% of what I own could be gone.”

2. Why it measures loss, not probability

A pure probability, such as “the chance this institution fails”, has two problems:

  • It doesn't fit volatile assets. A Nigerian stock rarely “fails”, but it can lose 40% of its value. A Canadian GIC almost never loses anything. Both need to sit on the same scale.
  • Probabilities of different events can't be averaged into anything meaningful. Lost value can: if 10% of your money is 50% exposed, then 5% of the total is at risk.

So every factor answers one question: what fraction of this asset's USD value is lost in a bad year?

3. Scoring one asset

Each asset passes through layers. The layers combine by compounding: each one takes its share of whatever value is left after the layers above it.

asset_risk = 1 − (1 − world) × (1 − country) × (1 − institution) × (1 − asset_type)
  • If the country layer is 1.0 (war or total collapse), the asset's risk is 1.0 no matter what the other layers say.
  • If the country is safe, the institution and asset-type layers decide the score.
  • World is one global value applied to every asset. It sits near 0 in normal times, and you raise it during a global crisis.

4. Layer 1: country (and world)

The country layer has three parts, which compound the same way:

country = 1 − (1 − sovereign) × (1 − currency) × (1 − convertibility)
PartWhat it capturesWhere the number comes from
Sovereign / political Government default, unrest, war Credit rating converted to a stress loss (AAA ≈ 0%, B ≈ 3–5%). A war/collapse switch forces the country to 1.0.
Currency Depreciation against USD The higher of a long-run minimum set in admin (NGN 40%, CAD 10%) and the 95th-percentile one-year move calculated from the app's exchange-rate history. The app's history starts in Feb 2025, too short and calm to see a 1-in-20 year, so the minimum usually applies.
Convertibility Money that can't be moved or converted out of the country Set by hand: NG a few %, CA ≈ 0.
Currency follows the asset's currency, not its country. A USD account held in Nigeria (e.g. Axa Mansard USD) takes Nigeria's sovereign and convertibility risk, but no naira depreciation.

5. Layer 2: institution

For each holder (Scotia, RBC, UBA, FirstBank, Axa Mansard, OminiPay, …):

institution = chance of failure × share lost if it fails
  • Chance of failure depends on the institution's tier: systemically important bank < ordinary bank < asset manager < fintech.
  • Share lost if it fails is reduced by deposit insurance. CDIC covers C$100k per category per member bank; NDIC has its own cap (check the current limit).
  • The insured amount is worked out across all your accounts at the same holder, so putting too much with one institution raises the risk automatically. No separate concentration rule is needed.
  • Fund managers keep client assets in separate custody, so less is lost if the manager itself fails.
  • This layer applies only to savings and investments, where you are owed money by the holder. For stocks, businesses and property the “holder” is the thing you own, so its risk is already in the asset-type layer.
  • Holders with no row in admin get a cautious default (5% failure × 70% lost) and are flagged as unrated.

6. Layer 3: asset type

This is the risk of the instrument itself, separate from where it's held:

7. Rolling it up into one number

portfolio_risk = Σ (asset_USD_value × asset_risk) / Σ asset_USD_value

This assumes all the bad things happen in the same year. That errs on the cautious side, which is realistic here because the Nigerian assets all move with the same naira shock.

8. Worked example

Illustrative numbers only. The real values come from the calibration table.

NGN fixed deposit at Axa Mansard
  • Country: sovereign 4%, currency 40%, convertibility 5% → 1 − 0.96 × 0.60 × 0.95 = 45.3%
  • Institution: asset manager, 3% failure × 50% lost = 1.5%
  • Asset type: FD = 0%
  • Asset risk: 1 − 0.547 × 0.985 = 46.1%
CAD GIC at Scotia Bank
  • Country: sovereign 0%, currency (CAD vs USD) 10%, convertibility 0% → 10%
  • Institution: systemically important bank, inside CDIC cover → ≈ 0%
  • Asset type: GIC = 0%
  • Asset risk: 10%
Portfolio: 60% in the GIC, 40% in the FD
0.60 × 10% + 0.40 × 46.1% = 24.4% → Moderate

9. Keeping it reliable

  • Calculate what can be calculated (currency stress, insurance coverage) from the app's own data, instead of guessing.
  • Keep the hand-set values in the Django admin, under Networth: Country risks (sovereign, convertibility, war switch; the WORLD row is the global factor), Currency risks, Institution risks and Asset type risks. Each row records a source and a last-reviewed date, and the dashboard shows a warning icon when a row is more than 6 months old.
  • Back-test it: the app's own exchange-rate history starts in Feb 2025, after the 2023–24 naira devaluation, so the NGN minimum was set from that event (roughly a 40%+ loss in USD terms). Check it again whenever the naira moves sharply.
  • Keep Liquidity separate. It has its own dashboard slot, and blending it into Risk would muddy both numbers.

10. On the dashboard

  • The Risk slot shows the portfolio score and its band.
  • A tooltip breaks it down by layer (e.g. “Country 18 · Institution 3 · Asset type 6”) and lists the three assets contributing the most risk.
  • The naira-breach probability that used to fill this slot now appears in the tooltip. The info icon links to this page, and a warning icon appears when values are unrated or overdue for review.

11. Decisions

  • Base currency: USD. Matches every other dashboard total, so a CAD fall against USD counts as risk.
  • Horizon: 1 year. The score reacts to current conditions.
  • Hand-set values: admin only. AI-suggested updates can be added later.
